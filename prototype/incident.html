First-hour incident card

Business email compromise is among the most reported cybercrimes against Australian business, and settlement-day payment redirection is the version that hits conveyancing practices hardest (ASD 2025). The two mistakes small firms make under pressure are ringing a number from the fraudulent email, and deleting it afterwards. This card names both.

If something goes wrong: the first hour

Suspected payment redirection

Someone has changed bank details by email, or a client says the money never arrived. Work down this list in order. It should take under an hour.

01 Ring the bank on the number on your card, not one from an email. Ask for the fraud team and say the words “misdirected payment”. Recall is possible for a short window and it shrinks by the hour.
02 Do not delete anything. Preserve the email and its headers. Move it to a folder if you must, but keep it. The headers are what tell an investigator whether the mailbox itself was reached.
03 Change the password on the affected mailbox, then check forwarding rules. An attacker who has been reading mail usually leaves a rule behind. Changing the password without removing the rule fixes nothing.
04 Tell the other side of the transaction by phone. Use a number you already held, not one from the message. If they have also paid, their clock is running too.
05 Notifiable data breach? Assess within 30 days, and call us. If client identity information may have been reached, the assessment clock starts now. Report cybercrime at cyber.gov.au/report.

Never, under any circumstances

  • Ring a phone number that appears in the suspicious email.
  • Delete the email, the sent items, or the mailbox rules.
  • Send further funds while you are still working out what happened.
